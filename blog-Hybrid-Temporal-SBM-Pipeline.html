From One Observed Network to Many Plausible Panels

Published on 10 Mar 2026 18 min read

TL;DR

  • The paper does not treat one generated network as a replacement for the observed Dutch livestock movement panel. Instead, it treats the observed panel as one sparse empirical realization, fits a layered nested stochastic block model, and generates an ensemble of posterior-predictive replicas.[1][5][6][8][10]
  • The representation is hybrid: farms in one focal region remain explicit, while the rest of the country is contracted to COROP supernodes. That creates four interpretable movement channels: F→F, F→R, R→F, and R→R.[4][14]
  • Validation happens on two levels. First, the workflow compares observed and synthetic panels using structural and higher-order statistics such as TEA/TNA, π-mass, and magnetic spectra. Second, it runs a farm-centred transmission stress test to check whether the synthetic panels preserve spread-relevant structure.[9][10][11][12][13]

Most network-generation stories skip directly from “here is the observed graph” to “here is a synthetic one”. This paper takes a more careful route. We start from a single observed Dutch livestock movement panel, build a hybrid temporal network around one focal region, fit a layered nested SBM, generate many plausible replicas, and ask whether those replicas preserve the structure that matters for downstream inference. That logic is deeply aligned with temporal-network theory, posterior-predictive model checking, and the way livestock movement data are actually used in epidemiology.[1][2][3][8][10]

The whole pipeline at a glance

The methodology is easiest to understand as a six-stage loop: observe one temporal panel, contract it into a hybrid graph, attach realized-edge annotations, fit a layered nested model, generate many synthetic panels, and then validate them with both network diagnostics and a propagation-based reality check.

Concept sketch: end-to-end workflow

This is a conceptual workflow figure rather than a results plot. The key message is that the pipeline is posterior-predictive: one observed temporal network goes in, an ensemble of plausible replicated panels comes out, and those replicas are evaluated rather than accepted by default.[8][9][10]
Code map: pipeline.py handles preprocessing, graph construction, model fitting, posterior refresh, and synthetic generation. diagnostics.py computes overlap, turnover, channel composition, π-mass, and magnetic diagnostics. simulation.py applies the hybrid-aware transmission stress test to the observed and generated panels.

Start with one observed temporal network, not with a synthetic world

The paper’s framing is important. We usually observe a single administrative realization of a livestock movement system, not repeated copies of the same underlying process. But the questions we care about—imports into a region, edge turnover, directional flow structure, and outbreak behaviour—are all probabilistic. Temporal-network theory tells us why this matters: the ordering of edges changes causal reachability and can change downstream dynamics even when the time-aggregated graph looks similar.[1]

That is why the pipeline is built around posterior-predictive augmentation. The observed panel is treated as one sparse empirical realization; the fitted model is then asked to generate a family of plausible alternative panels; and the scientific question becomes whether the ensemble preserves the structures we care about. This is exactly the spirit of posterior-predictive model criticism in Bayesian workflow and of modern goodness-of-fit assessment for network models.[8][9][10]

1. Hybridize the network without throwing away context

A fully premise-level national network gives you detail everywhere, but often at the cost of interpretation and computational focus. This study cares about one focal Dutch region while still needing the national backdrop that drives imports, exports, and indirect connectivity. The solution is a hybrid temporal graph: keep focal farms explicit, contract every non-focal premise into a COROP-level regional supernode.[4][14]

Formally, if $c^*$ is the focal COROP, the contraction map is $$ \phi(i)= \begin{cases} i, & i\in F,\\ c(i), & i\notin F, \end{cases} $$ where $F$ is the set of focal farms and $c(i)$ is the non-focal COROP containing premise $i$. That one choice gives the whole hybrid semantics of the paper: F→F for within-region movement, F→R for exports, R→F for imports, and R→R for external background flow.

Concept sketch: full premise graph vs hybrid contraction

Left: a premise-level view. Right: the same system after applying the hybrid contraction map. Focal farms remain explicit, non-focal premises collapse into regional supernodes, parallel flows are aggregated, and within-region external traffic becomes a self-loop on the supernode. This is why the hybrid representation preserves boundary flux while shrinking the outside world.[4][14]
Interpretation note. The hybrid graph is not saying that a region supernode is “one giant farm”. It is saying that the outside world is represented at a coarser epidemiological and statistical resolution than the focal region.

2. Attach realized-edge annotations and fit one shared hierarchy across days

After date filtering and simplification, the code attaches deterministic realized-edge annotations such as centroid distance, gravity-like terms derived from farm and animal counts, optional feature cosine similarity, and calendar descriptors. The important methodological detail is that these variables are attached to edges that actually occurred. They are not evaluated over all possible dyads, so they should be read as observed edge annotations informing inference rather than as a standalone edge-formation regression.

The fitted model is a degree-corrected layered nested SBM. “Layered” means daily snapshots are treated as linked layers; “nested” means block structure is learned hierarchically rather than fixed at one resolution; and “shared hierarchy” means the model looks for persistent mesoscale organization across the whole panel rather than fitting each day in isolation.[5][6] In the implementation, farm and region node types are also constrained so the model does not merge those semantics into the same block.

Concept sketch: daily layers, one latent hierarchy, many draws

The point is not that day 1, day 2, and day 3 are identical. The point is that they are interpreted through one shared latent hierarchy. Once that hierarchy is fitted, the workflow can optionally refresh the partition locally and generate multiple plausible panels from the same fitted model.[5][6][10]

3. Let the data choose a weight family

If weights are available, the pipeline does not silently assume one observation family. Instead it builds a support-compatible candidate set and chooses the best one by description length. Integer-valued weights can be compared under discrete Poisson and discrete geometric families. Non-negative continuous weights can be compared under a raw-scale exponential model or a transformed Gaussian model. Unrestricted continuous weights fall back to Gaussian.[7]

That choice matters because weight modelling is part of the generative story, not just a preprocessing afterthought. In other words, the model is not only learning whether an edge exists on a given day; it is also learning how movement intensity should be represented.

4. Regenerate weights with channel-aware backoff instead of one global fallback

Synthetic topology is sampled first. Then synthetic edge weights are drawn conditionally. Ideally, a new weight would be drawn from the exact day-and-block cell that matches the sampled edge. But exact cells are often sparse in real temporal panels. The elegant part of the implementation is the channel-aware backoff hierarchy: $$ (t,r,s) \rightarrow (r,s) \rightarrow (t,\tau_u,\tau_v) \rightarrow (\tau_u,\tau_v) \rightarrow (t) \rightarrow \text{global}. $$ Here $(r,s)$ is the sampled block pair and $(\tau_u,\tau_v)$ is the ordered hybrid edge channel.

This matters because an $F\to F$ edge and an $R\to F$ edge do not usually have the same typical intensity. If sparse cells were shrunk directly to one global mean, the synthetic panel would blur precisely the hybrid structure we care about.

Concept sketch: channel-aware weight backoff

minimum support 3 exact-cell support 2
With the default threshold, sparse exact cells borrow strength from the next best supported level. The crucial middle levels preserve hybrid channel semantics, so the sampler can remain conservative without collapsing all movement intensities to the same network-wide baseline.

5. Generate an ensemble, not a single substitute network

Once the model is fitted, synthetic panels are sampled snapshot by snapshot on the same daily layer grid. The code can optionally refresh the partition by local MCMC sweeps before generation, which lets repeated synthetic panels explore partition uncertainty near the fitted optimum. In the main workflow, multiple topological samplers and rewiring-based sensitivity settings can then be crossed to form a grid of generated scenarios.

The important interpretive point is that setting-level summaries are posterior summaries over repeated generated panels, not claims about one internally privileged synthetic graph. That is exactly the right mindset for posterior-predictive checking: the question is whether the ensemble reproduces the observed descriptors, not whether one sample can mimic every edge perfectly.[8][9][10]

6. Validate on two levels: structure and spread

The validation stack is broader than a typical degree-distribution check. At the first layer, diagnostics.py compares observed and synthetic panels directly with per-snapshot overlap, activity, density, reciprocity, weight totals, and novelty. Then it moves to temporal turnover via TEA and TNA, which ask whether the model gets persistence, new activation, reactivation, and churn right.

After that come the higher-order checks: stationary mass under PageRank-like random walks and direction-sensitive magnetic Laplacian spectra. These help test whether the synthetic ensemble preserves flow concentration and directional organization, not only edge counts.[11][12][13]

This is an illustrative dashboard, not an empirical figure from the paper. It shows how the workflow stacks direct panel comparison, higher-order temporal diagnostics, and a spread-oriented stress test instead of relying on a single goodness-of-fit number.[9][10][12][13]

7. The transmission “reality check” is about spread-relevant structure, not forecasting

The final step is where the pipeline becomes more than a network-fitting exercise. In simulation.py, farms are the true epidemic units and region supernodes are treated as external pressure reservoirs. That distinction is crucial. A region node is not interpreted as an infected mega-premise. It is a coarse state summarizing import pressure from the contracted outside world.

The simulator assigns separate channel-specific multipliers to the four hybrid edge classes, supports SIR/SIS/SEIR-style farm dynamics, and applies matched seeding to observed and synthetic panels. The main reported endpoints are farm-based: attack rate, peak prevalence, peak timing, outbreak duration, and time to the first newly infected farm beyond the seed set. That makes the simulation a propagation-based validation layer, not a calibrated national forecast.[2][3]

Why this is a strong design choice. Structural agreement alone does not guarantee epidemiological agreement. The reality-check simulator asks a narrower and more defensible question: if two temporal panels are fed into the same channel-aware farm-centred transmission rule, do they induce similar farm-level outbreak behaviour?

Why this pipeline matters

What makes the methodology interesting is not any one component in isolation. Hybrid graph contraction, layered SBMs, weighted SBMs, posterior-predictive checks, and propagation models all exist separately in the literature. What this paper does is wire them together into one coherent workflow: compress the network without losing the focal question, fit a model that respects temporal and multiscale structure, generate an ensemble instead of a single replacement, and evaluate that ensemble with both network-science and epidemiological diagnostics.[4][5][6][7][10]

In plain language, the pipeline asks a better question than “does the synthetic graph look similar?”. It asks: does the model preserve the structures that matter for the scientific question? For a focal-region livestock movement network, that is exactly the question worth asking.
